### A Practical Approach: The Guillotine Algorithm

Given your goal to minimize the number of cuts and eventually produce files similar to Ardis, a **guillotine-cut algorithm** is the perfect model. This means that every cut goes from one edge of a rectangle all the way to the opposite edge, creating two smaller rectangular areas. This is simpler than allowing free-form placement and naturally limits the number of cuts.

A good way to implement this is with a **recursive, best-fit heuristic**:

1.  **Start** with a single rectangle representing the whole sheet.
2.  **Select a Part:** Pick a part from the list of parts you need to cut.
3.  **Place the Part:** Place it in one of the corners of the sheet (e.g., the bottom-left).
4.  **Make Two Guillotine Cuts:** Make one horizontal and one vertical cut to "slice off" the part you just placed. This leaves you with two new, smaller empty rectangles.
5.  **Recurse:** Now you have a smaller list of parts and a list of two new empty rectangles. Repeat the process for each new rectangle, always choosing the placement that leaves the most "useful" leftover space.
6.  **Repeat** until no more parts can be placed. The remaining empty rectangles are your offcuts and scrap.

***

### Creating a Configurable "Optimization Score"

The best way to handle your configurable priorities is with a **weighted scoring function**. The goal of the algorithm will be to find a layout that maximizes this score.

You can define the score like this:
`Score = (w_yield * Yield%) + (w_offcut * Offcut_Score) - (w_cuts * Number_of_Cuts)`

* `w_yield`, `w_offcut`, and `w_cuts` are the **weights** you will make configurable in your app's UI. For example, setting `w_cuts` to a high number makes the algorithm prioritize solutions with fewer cuts.
* **Yield%** is the total area of the parts you placed, divided by the total area of the sheet.
* **Offcut_Score** is where you can be clever. Instead of just summing the area of usable offcuts, you can reward "good" shapes. A simple way to do this is to penalize long, thin pieces: `Score_for_one_offcut = (Area / Aspect_Ratio)`, where `Aspect_Ratio = max(L, W) / min(L, W)`. A square-like offcut will have a much higher score than a long, thin one of the same area.

***

### Other Important Factors to Consider

You asked if there was anything else to think about. Yes, here are the most critical factors for a real-world cutting application:

* **Kerf (Saw Blade Thickness):** This is the most important one. Every cut removes a small amount of material (e.g., 1/8" or 3mm). Your algorithm **must** account for this. When you make a cut, the total size of the two resulting pieces will be smaller than the original piece. `(Piece1_Width + Piece2_Width + Kerf) = Original_Width`. Ignoring this will result in all your parts being slightly too small.
* **Part Rotation:** Can parts be rotated by 90 degrees? Allowing rotation can dramatically improve yield but also increases the complexity of the search. This should be a configurable option (`Allow Rotation: Yes/No`).
* **Material Grain Direction:** For materials like wood, you can often only cut parts in one orientation due to the grain pattern. This acts as a constraint that would disable rotation for those specific parts.
* **Part Grouping:** When you have many identical parts, it's often more efficient to cut them from a single, larger block. Your algorithm could be designed to recognize this and cluster identical parts together.
